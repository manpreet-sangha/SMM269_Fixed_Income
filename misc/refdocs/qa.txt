Our group has the following questions regarding the interest rate model question (Q5) in the Fixed Income course work:
Would you mind clarifying the task about “pricing the option-like component of the bond” please? Does it mean that since the coupon for each period is non-linear, we treat the bond price as a series of option maturing at each coupon date and we price each option with the interest rate model? Answer: You’ll need to look closely at the coupon formula provided in the bond description. Your task is to decompose that formula into its elementary building blocks: the fixed component, the floating component, the optional (derivative) component, and the nominal. Once separated, you price each piece individually using the model. You can find several walkthroughs of this process in the Exercise Book, Part II, Section VIII: Coupon Decomposition.
May we know if we are supposed to model the 3M EURIBOR (i.e., the underlying of the bond coupons) using a model calibrated with 6M EURIBOR caplets? Answer: This is a key analytical hurdle for your group to address. You need to decide on a methodology for handling the tenor basis (the discrepancy between 3M and 6M rates and the fact that you have data on the &M rates) and justify your choice. There isn't a single "correct" answer, but your report should clearly explain the logic behind your approach..
Is there any restriction on our model choice? (e.g., are we supposed to choose short rate models or forward rate/market models?) Answer: Your choice is largely guided by the data provided. I have supplied volatility surfaces for both Bachelier (Normal) and Displaced Black models. While you have freedom in your selection, remember that every assumption must be rigorously justified. Think of it this way: you should be prepared to defend your pricing results and model logic as if you were presenting to a sophisticated institutional investor. A simplified example of pricing in again in the Exercise Book, Part II, Section X - Pricing a Structured Bond.
Should we use the interest rate model calibrated in Q5 in the rest of questions such as computing CVA, sensitivities and VaR?  Answer Consistency is vital. You should use the same model calibrated in Q5 for the subsequent risk calculations. Switching models between different tasks is difficult to defend professionally; it can appear as "model shopping" to achieve a more favorable result. Stick to a unified framework to ensure your risk metrics (CVA, VaR) are coherent with your pricing.
About Question 3. Bootstrapping. We are not really sure about how to apply the data given to us. We understand that bootstrapping is used because the market data that we have does not allow us to create the yield curve. So, there are different instruments in the market that possess some information that we can get in order to build the yield curve. Let's say we have the rate of a 3-month deposit; we can have a discount factor from t= 0 to t = 3m and so on with the instruments in the market. Is this the right idea for bootstrapping? What data provided is the one that we have to use?
Answer The bootstrapping procedure for Euribor deposits and swap rates was covered in the first two weeks of lectures and is described in the PDF file titled Couon Bond (par rates), Bootstrapping and Interpolation and was also covered by presenting an Excel exampl. You should refer to that material, as it provides the methodology expected for this exercise.
In the Excel file Market Data, there is a sheet named IRS CURVE. In that sheet you will find the Euribor deposit rates and the swap rates quoted on the pricing date. Those are the market inputs you must use to construct the discount curve.
The logic you describe is correct at a conceptual level. A short term deposit rate, for example 3 months, allows you to compute the discount factor from time zero to 3 months. Then, using longer maturities such as swaps, you sequentially extract the remaining discount factors. This recursive extraction of discount factors from market instruments is precisely what bootstrapping means in this context. However, for the coursework you are expected to apply the methodology presented in class, using the data provided in the IRS CURVE sheet and the correct day count convention as well as to consider in an appopriate way holidays and weekends.

About Question 5.  Model. We have three different models, and each model is used for different assumptions. In order to select the model, we should price the instrument given the data and compare the price with the market price to select the correct model?
Answer:  You are not asked to try all models and then mechanically select the one that gives the closest price. Instead, you must assess which model is appropriate given the characteristics of the instrument and the assumptions required. Once you select the model, you must implement it and justify your choice. The justification should refer to the underlying assumptions, for example about the dynamics of the forward rate, volatility surface, and explain why that framework is suitable for the problem at hand. The adopted model must therefore be both implemented correctly and theoretically motivated.
